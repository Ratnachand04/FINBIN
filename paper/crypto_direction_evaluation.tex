\begin{abstract}
We report a two-stage failure that we believe is common in applied trading
research, and we quantify both stages on a single system. In the first stage the
measurement apparatus is wrong. Auditing a complete cryptocurrency trading
pipeline that reported an aggregate annualised Sharpe ratio of $4.54$ and a
per-series maximum of $23.65$, we isolate twelve code-level faults arising from
five root causes, spanning execution, metric computation and the feature
pipeline. Among them is a feature named for sentiment that is in fact a scaled,
noised copy of the contemporaneous label, correlating $0.916$ with the same day
return and $-0.018$ with the next day. Every fault is reproduced, corrected, and
pinned by a regression test; a mutation study reintroducing each fault kills
$8/8$ of the mutants it can express. In the second stage, which survives the
first, the model is compared against the wrong baseline. Re-running the study
under a corrected harness with expanding-window walk-forward validation, a
purge-and-embargo gap and per-fold scaler fitting, a regularised linear model
attains $52.95\%$ next-day directional accuracy over $5{,}562$ out-of-sample
predictions ($p < 10^{-4}$ against the coin flip). The same data yields
$52.88\%$ from inverse persistence -- a zero-parameter rule that bets against
yesterday's sign. Paired tests on identical days give differences of
$-0.29$, $+0.53$ and $-0.07$ percentage points on BTC, ETH and DOGE, none
remotely significant, with the model losing on two of three assets. Thirty-four
engineered features, per-fold scaling and walk-forward machinery recover a
well-documented one-day reversal effect and nothing beyond it. The effect is
also not tradable: gross annualised Sharpe is $0.544$ with a stationary-bootstrap
$95\%$ interval of $[-0.17, +1.28]$ that contains zero, and the appraisal ratio
against a passive basket is $-0.076$. A decomposition attributes the
frictionless-to-executed gap to transaction costs ($-0.43$ Sharpe) and a
holding-period mismatch ($-0.14$), and shows the entry lag costs essentially
nothing, contradicting a common intuition imported from equities into a market
that trades continuously. We propose mutation scoring and a randomised null test
as minimum verification for backtested claims, and show that the single-path
version of that null test which we previously advocated is itself uncalibrated,
producing a positive Sharpe on $13.3\%$ of correct-engine draws.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

Consider two ways a trading result can be wrong. In the first, the code that
computes performance does not compute what it claims to; the reported number is
an artefact of the instrument. In the second, the code is correct but the
comparison is uninformative, because the proposed method is measured against a
null so weak that beating it establishes nothing. The first failure is a bug.
The second is a research-design error, and it is the harder of the two to see,
because everything reported is technically true.

This paper documents both, on the same system, in sequence. We began with a
working cryptocurrency trading pipeline whose published evaluation claimed an
aggregate annualised Sharpe ratio of $4.54$ across BTC, ETH and DOGE, with
individual series reaching $23.65$, alongside a $30.23\%$ win rate against a
nominal $+2\%$ target and $-1.5\%$ stop.

\subsection{A first indication}

Under the stated barrier levels, a strategy exiting only at its target or stop
would have expectancy
\begin{equation}
0.3023 \times 2\% \;-\; 0.6977 \times 1.5\% \;=\; -0.44\%
\label{eq:contradiction}
\end{equation}
per trade before costs, which is inconsistent with a Sharpe ratio of $4.54$.

This calculation is suggestive rather than decisive, and we state the limitation
because it bears on how the paper should be read. The audited system also
carried a wall-clock timeout exit. Trades closing on timeout land at neither
barrier, so win rate and expectancy decouple and \eqref{eq:contradiction} ceases
to bind. The reported figures were therefore not provably contradictory from the
table alone -- only implausible enough to justify opening the code. Our first
revision of this manuscript asserted the contradiction outright; that was an
overstatement, and correcting it is one of several self-corrections recorded in
Section~\ref{ssec:ourerrors}.

\subsection{What we found, in two stages}

\paragraph{Stage one: the instrument.} We isolate twelve code-level faults
(Section~\ref{sec:defects}) arising from five root causes. They include
look-ahead in the fill path, cross-asset contamination of position pricing,
annualisation of per-trade returns by a fixed factor, sign-inverted
mark-to-market for shorts, a Monte Carlo routine that was permutation-invariant
and therefore constant, and a feature scaler fitted on a single row at inference
so that every served feature vector was identically zero. Separately, a feature
presented as sentiment proves to be a transform of the contemporaneous label
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).

\paragraph{Stage two: the baseline.} With the instrument corrected, the
re-measured result is $52.95\%$ directional accuracy over $5{,}562$
out-of-sample predictions, $p < 10^{-4}$ against a coin flip. This is the number
our first revision reported as the finding. It is not a finding. On the same
days, \emph{inverse persistence} -- predict the opposite of yesterday's
direction, a rule with no parameters and no inputs beyond the previous sign --
scores $52.88\%$. Paired comparisons on identical observations give differences
of $-0.29$, $+0.53$ and $-0.07$ percentage points on BTC, ETH and DOGE, with
$|t| \le 0.45$ throughout. The machine-learning model loses on two of three
assets and is indistinguishable from the trivial rule on the third.

The substantive content of the result is therefore that daily cryptocurrency
returns exhibit one-day reversal, which is neither novel nor ours. What thirty-four
engineered features and walk-forward machinery add over a one-line rule is,
within measurement error, zero.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A fault taxonomy with mutation scoring.} Twelve code-level
    faults from five root causes, each with mechanism, correction and test. We
    verify coverage by reintroducing each fault and confirming the suite turns
    red, reporting a mutation score rather than a test count
    (Sections~\ref{sec:defects}, \ref{ssec:mutation}).

    \item \textbf{A leakage case study of a disguised label}
    (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}), including why it evades distributional leakage
    detection and a provenance test that catches it.

    \item \textbf{A baseline-corrected re-evaluation} showing that the
    machine-learning result does not separate from inverse persistence, with
    paired tests, Pesaran--Timmermann statistics, Wilson intervals and per-fold
    stability (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

    \item \textbf{An economic assessment with honest uncertainty}: book-level
    $\mu$, $\sigma$ and turnover, stationary-bootstrap intervals on Sharpe, a
    breakeven cost \emph{with} a confidence interval, and an appraisal ratio
    against a passive basket rather than a raw Sharpe comparison
    (Sections~\ref{ssec:book}--\ref{ssec:bench}).

    \item \textbf{An attribution ladder} decomposing the frictionless-to-executed
    gap one change at a time (Section~\ref{ssec:ladder}), which overturns the
    timing-based explanation we previously gave.

    \item \textbf{A calibrated null-test protocol} (Section~\ref{ssec:null}),
    together with the demonstration that its single-path form -- which we
    previously proposed -- is uncalibrated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope, and what we do not claim}

We audit one system. We do \emph{not} claim to have established the prevalence
of these faults in the published literature, and the reader should not infer it:
a single artefact, selected for being available to us, cannot support a claim
about a field. The natural follow-up -- a prevalence study across a sample of
public repositories and published replication packages -- is described in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} and is not attempted here.

We also do not propose a novel architecture. The models are deliberately
conventional, because the paper is about measurement, and a novel architecture
evaluated on an uncalibrated harness is uninformative about the architecture.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Backtest overfitting and multiplicity}

\citet{bailey2014deflated} formalise the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, discounting an
observed Sharpe by the number of configurations tried and by non-normality.
\citet{harvey2016cross} argue the conventional $t > 2$ threshold is badly
miscalibrated given the number of factors tested. \citet{hou2020replicating}
replicate several hundred anomalies and find a majority fail under careful
methodology. \citet{lopezdeprado2018advances} supplies the methodological
toolkit we use most: purging and embargoing, and the argument that standard
cross-validation is invalid for financial series. \citet{arnott2019backtesting}
propose a backtesting protocol for machine learning.

These works largely assume the engine computes what it claims and address the
inference layered on top. We examine the engine, where the errors are not subtle
biases but implementation faults with large effects, and we add a verification
step -- mutation testing -- that is standard in software engineering
\citep{jia2011analysis} and, to our knowledge, unreported in this setting.

\subsection{Data snooping and reality checks}

\citet{white2000reality} introduced the Reality Check bootstrap;
\citet{sullivan1999data} applied it to technical trading rules and found
apparent profitability largely vanishes once snooping is accounted for.
\citet{hansen2005test} refines this into the Test for Superior Predictive
Ability. \citet{politis1994stationary} supply the stationary bootstrap we use
for Sharpe intervals under serial dependence.

\subsection{Baselines and directional testing}

The methodological point at the centre of our result is old.
\citet{makridakis2018statistical} document that sophisticated methods routinely
fail to beat simple benchmarks once evaluation is disciplined, and that the
benchmark is frequently omitted. \citet{pesaran1992simple} give the standard
test of directional predictive accuracy, which tests independence of predicted
and realised direction rather than a hit rate against $0.5$; we report it
throughout. \citet{diebold1995comparing} give the paired framework for comparing
two forecasts on the same observations, which is the correct instrument for
model-versus-baseline comparison and which unpaired confidence intervals do not
provide.

\subsection{Short-horizon reversal}

Short-horizon return reversal is long documented in equities
\citep{lo1990contrarian, jegadeesh1990evidence} and reported in cryptocurrency
\citep{caporale2019reverse}. Our contribution here is not the effect but the
observation that a machine-learning pipeline evaluated without the corresponding
baseline will recover it and present it as learned structure.

\subsection{Leakage and machine learning for returns}

\citet{kaufman2012leakage} give the canonical treatment of leakage.
\citet{bergmeir2012use} and \citet{cerqueira2020evaluating} examine
cross-validation validity for time series. \citet{gu2020empirical} provide the
reference comparison of machine learning for asset returns, finding modest gains
and that regularised linear methods are competitive with far more flexible
models -- consistent with our finding that logistic regression beats
gradient-boosted trees on every asset. \citet{fama1970efficient} and
\citet{timmermann2004efficient} supply the null: predictability may exist but
should be small, unstable, and bounded by costs.

\section{Fault Taxonomy}
\label{sec:defects}
%==============================================================================

We count twelve code-level faults, but they are not twelve independent
discoveries, and presenting them as such would overstate the finding. They arise
from five root causes, given in Table~\ref{tab:defects}. We report both counts:
the fault count reflects what a maintainer must fix, the root-cause count
reflects what a reviewer should learn.

We flag two honest qualifications. First, F12 is a labelling error, not a
computation error: the quantity was computed correctly and named misleadingly.
Second, an additional fault in the original code -- imputation using a
full-sample column mean -- has essentially no effect on Binance daily OHLCV,
which contains no missing values in our sample. We corrected it for
generality but exclude it from the twelve because we cannot demonstrate an
effect, and including unquantified items is the practice this paper criticises.

\subsection{R1: timing}

\paragraph{F1, look-ahead in the price lookup.} The resolver selected the bar
minimising $|t_{\text{bar}} - t_{\text{signal}}|$, which for any signal not on a
bar boundary can return a bar that has not occurred. Corrected to a binary
search over $\{i : t_i \le t_{\text{signal}}\}$.

\paragraph{F2, absent implementation lag.} Signals derived from the close of bar
$i$ were filled at that same close. Corrected to fill at the open of bar $i+1$.
Section~\ref{ssec:ladder} shows this correction is worth approximately zero on
crypto data, for reasons given there.

\paragraph{F3, holding-window mismatch.} The corrected engine initially used a
wall-clock timeout. On daily bars a position entered at the open of $t+1$ cannot
close before bar $t+2$, so it realises $O_{t+1} \to C_{t+2}$: two days of
exposure against a one-day forecast. We introduce an explicit bar-counted
holding period, so that a one-period label is traded over one period.
\emph{This fault was present in our own corrected engine} and is discussed in
Section~\ref{ssec:ourerrors}.

\subsection{R2: accounting}

\paragraph{F4, cross-asset pricing.} The loop iterated over signals, resolved one
price per iteration, and passed it to the exit check for every open position
regardless of asset, so a Bitcoin position could be stopped out at Dogecoin
price. Corrected by iterating over bars and pricing each position from its own
series.

\paragraph{F5, inverted short mark-to-market.} Portfolio value summed
$q_j p_t^{(j)}$ over all positions, so a short gained equity as price rose.
Corrected to
\begin{equation}
V_t = \text{cash}_t + \sum_{j \in \text{long}} q_j p_t^{(j)} - \sum_{j \in \text{short}} q_j p_t^{(j)}.
\label{eq:equity}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{F6, fee splitting.} The round-trip fee was divided evenly between
legs, so cash charged diverged from the fee reported whenever price moved. Each
leg is now charged on its own notional.

\subsection{R3: statistics}

\paragraph{F7, annualisation of per-trade returns.} Sharpe was computed as
$\bar{r}/\sigma_r \times \sqrt{252}$ on \emph{per-trade} returns. Trades do not
arrive on a fixed clock, so no such factor is defined. This is the largest single
contributor to the reported $23.65$. Corrected to
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{\overline{r - r_f}}{\sigma_{r - r_f}}\sqrt{P},
\qquad
P = \frac{\text{seconds per year}}{\mathrm{median}\,\{t_{i+1} - t_i\}},
\label{eq:sharpe}
\end{equation}
on equity-curve returns. A second implementation elsewhere in the same codebase
hardcoded $P = 35040$ and applied it to a per-signal-event curve, so the two
implementations disagreed with each other as well as with the definition.

\paragraph{F8, permutation-invariant Monte Carlo.} The routine shuffled the P\&L
vector and summed it. Summation is invariant under permutation, so every
simulated path returned an identical value, the interval collapsed to a point,
and the reported probability of profit was always exactly $0$ or $1$. Corrected
to resampling with replacement, additionally tracking the order-dependent
drawdown that motivates the exercise.

\paragraph{F9, Calmar denominator.} Cumulative return was divided by the worst
single trade rather than peak-to-trough drawdown, which the same module computed
correctly elsewhere and did not use.

\subsection{R4: statistics fitted on the wrong sample}

\paragraph{F10, single-row scaler.} At inference the feature vector was
standardised by \texttt{StandardScaler().fit\_transform(X)} on one row. With one
sample per column the mean equals the observation and the variance is zero, so
the output is identically zero and every served prediction ran on a zero vector.
Fitting is now separated from transformation and the fitted object persisted with
the model.

\paragraph{F11, split without embargo.} Samples were $60$-step windows with
next-bar labels and rolling features up to $672$ bars, split by a plain index
cut, so windows adjacent to the boundary shared bars across it. Corrected to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}} = [0, k), \quad
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}} = [k + e, n), \quad
e \ge L_{\text{seq}} + h,
\label{eq:embargo}
\end{equation}
following \citet{lopezdeprado2018advances}.

\subsection{R5: naming}

\paragraph{F12.} The trainer reported \texttt{sharpe\_ratio} and
\texttt{profit\_factor} built by assigning $\pm 1$ to correct and incorrect
predictions. For accuracy $a$ these are deterministic transforms,
$(2a-1)/(2\sqrt{a(1-a)})$ and $a/(1-a)$, carrying no information beyond $a$ while
presenting as risk and profitability measures.

\subsection{Why the faults survived}

Several would be caught by inspection individually. What preserved them is that
they \emph{compensate}. F5 corrupts the equity curve, but F7 computed Sharpe from
the trade record rather than the curve, so the corruption never surfaced in the
headline. F10 zeroed the served features, but reported accuracy came from an
offline path that did not use the serving code. Each published metric was
computed by a path that did not exercise the fault most likely to be noticed.
This is the argument for testing the instrument directly rather than inspecting
outputs for plausibility.

\section{Case Study: A Feature That Was the Label}

\subsection{Mechanism}

The pipeline consumed a per-asset daily sentiment series with fields
\texttt{sentiment\_score}, \texttt{sentiment\_label} and
\texttt{simulated\_headline}. The series is generated from the price data:
\begin{align}
r^{\text{same}}_t &= \frac{C_t - O_t}{O_t}, \\
s_t &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(15\, r^{\text{same}}_t,\, -1,\, 1\right) + \epsilon_t,
\qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{U}(-0.2, 0.2),
\label{eq:sentgen}
\end{align}
with headline text drawn from fifteen fixed templates selected by the sign and
magnitude of $s_t$ (Figure~\ref{fig:leak}).

\toprule

\midrule
\texttt{sentiment\_score} vs.\ \textbf{same-day} return  & $0.916$  & $87.0\%$ \\

Two consequences. A model using $s_t$ to predict the sign of $r^{\text{same}}_t$
has near-direct access to its label and will report accuracy in the high
eighties. Using $s_t$ to predict $r_{t+1}$ is \emph{not} leakage in the formal
sense -- $s_t$ is known at the close of $t$ -- but is worthless, contributing
nothing beyond a momentum term under a name implying an independent information
source.

\subsection{Why it evades detection, and a test that catches it}

Standard leakage checks look for distribution shift across the train/test
boundary or implausibly high validation scores. Neither fires. The generator adds
enough noise that the mapping is not exactly invertible, and the feature has a
plausible name, range, accompanying text field and documented ingestion script.
The failure is one of \emph{provenance}, not of statistics, and provenance is not
visible in the feature matrix.

We therefore recommend a provenance test for any feature claimed to derive from
an exogenous source: an assertion in the test suite that the feature cannot be
reconstructed from contemporaneous market data above a threshold $R^2$. Such a
test is included in the released harness and fails on this feature.

The sentiment series is excluded from every result below, and this paper makes no
claim regarding sentiment-driven prediction.

\subsection{Features}

Thirty-four causal features, each a function of bars indexed $\le t$: multi-horizon
log returns; rolling volatility and close $z$-scores; bar-shape measures; order
flow from Binance kline fields (taker buy ratio, average trade size, trade count,
volume, each $z$-scored or ratioed); and RSI, MACD histogram, Bollinger position,
ATR and a day-of-week encoding. Definitions are in \ref{app:features}.

We emphasise that this is a \emph{single-asset technical} feature set. It
excludes everything specific to crypto microstructure that plausibly carries
signal: perpetual funding rates, open interest, basis, liquidation cascades,
order-book imbalance, exchange netflows and cross-asset lead--lag. Conclusions
should be read as applying to this feature set, not to crypto forecasting
generally.

\subsection{Labels and validation}

The label is $y_t = \mathbf{1}[C_{t+1} > C_t]$, binary with no dead-band so that
the null is an unambiguous $50\%$. Validation is expanding-window walk-forward
with a $5$-day embargo, $1{,}000$-day initial train and $250$-day test folds
(Figure~\ref{fig:wf}). The scaler is fitted \emph{inside each fold on training
rows only}.

\subsection{Models and baselines}

Models: $\ell_2$-regularised logistic regression ($C = 0.1$) and histogram
gradient-boosted trees (depth $3$, $200$ iterations, learning rate $0.05$).
Hyperparameters were fixed a priori and not tuned on test data.

Baselines, all evaluated on identical days:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Persistence}: $\hat{y}_t = y_{t-1}$.
\item \textbf{Inverse persistence}: $\hat{y}_t = 1 - y_{t-1}$. With continuous
prices there are no ties, so its accuracy is exactly $1$ minus persistence. This
is the baseline whose omission invalidated our first revision.
\item \textbf{Majority}: the more frequent training-fold class.
\item \textbf{Buy-and-hold}: passive long exposure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical reporting}

We report Wilson score intervals rather than Wald, which is preferable near a
boundary and at moderate $n$. Against a fixed null $p_0$ we use the usual
proportion test. For model-versus-baseline we use a \emph{paired} test on the
per-observation difference of correctness indicators, following
\citet{diebold1995comparing}, because both forecasts are evaluated on identical
days and their errors are correlated; comparing two independent intervals is the
wrong instrument. We additionally report the \citet{pesaran1992simple} test,
which examines independence of predicted and realised direction rather than a hit
rate against $0.5$, and therefore does not credit a forecast for matching the
unconditional frequency of up moves.

Sharpe ratios carry stationary-bootstrap intervals \citep{politis1994stationary}
with mean block length $20$ days. Over samples this short an annualised Sharpe
without an interval is not interpretable, and its omission was a defect of our
first revision.

\subsection{Execution and cost model}

A signal from the close of day $t$ is filled at the open of $t+1$ and held one
bar, exiting at the close of $t+1$, so that a one-period label is traded over one
period (Figure~\ref{fig:timing}). Costs are $4$ bps taker fee plus $5$ bps
slippage per side, charged on the notional of that leg. We treat this cost level
as an assumption to be varied, not a fact, and Section~\ref{ssec:costs} reports
the whole sensitivity curve.

the right statistic.

\subsection{A calibrated null test}
\label{ssec:null}

Random entry signals on a driftless geometric random walk contain no structure by
construction, so a correct engine charging positive costs must lose at a rate set
by turnover and cost alone.

Our first revision proposed asserting $S \le 0$ on a single such path as a
publication standard. That proposal was itself uncalibrated. A $75$-day fixture
has $\mathrm{SE}(S) \approx \sqrt{1/0.2} \approx 2.2$, so the assertion is a
stochastic claim presented as deterministic. Running $60$ independent seeds
through the corrected engine, \textbf{$13.3\%$ of them produce a positive Sharpe}
(range $-10.45$ to $+7.33$). A single-path assertion would therefore fail on a
correct engine roughly one run in eight, and the single value of $-2.579$ that
revision reported sits about one standard error from zero.

The calibrated version tests the distribution:
\begin{equation}
\bar{S} = -3.534, \quad \mathrm{sd} = 3.643, \quad \mathrm{se} = 0.470,
\quad t = -7.51 .
\end{equation}

Two further assertions accompany it. Cash conservation,
\begin{equation}
V_T - V_0 = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathrm{PnL}_i ,
\label{eq:reconcile}
\end{equation}
holds to $1.2 \times 10^{-11}$ on runs that end flat, and is violated by any
fault creating or destroying cash. Realised fee rate matches the modelled
two-sided fee to within tolerance.

We drop a third assertion used previously -- that expectancy implied by win rate
and average win/loss equals mean return per trade -- because it is a tautology:
it decomposes a mean into conditional means and can fail only if the reporting
layer disagrees with itself. Presenting it as an independent check overstated the
protocol's strength.

\paragraph{Coverage, stated honestly.} This protocol exercises the \emph{engine}
with exogenous signals. It is blind to feature leakage, split leakage and
imputation leakage -- faults F10 and F11 and the entire case study of
Section~\ref{sec:leakage}. Describing it as a ``minimum publication requirement
for backtested claims,'' as our first revision did, overstated its coverage
considerably. It is one necessary check among several, complementary to
CSCV/PBO \citep{bailey2014deflated} and the SPA test \citep{hansen2005test},
neither of which it replaces.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Two failures, and which is worse}

The instrument failure inflated a Sharpe ratio from below zero to $23.65$. It is
dramatic, and it is also the easier of the two to prevent: it yields to fixtures
with known answers, mutation testing and null tests, all of which are mechanical.

The baseline failure is quieter. Every number our first revision reported was
correctly computed. The walk-forward was properly purged, the scaler properly
fitted, the costs properly charged, the $p$-value properly calculated. The
conclusion was still wrong, because the comparison was against a null that a
one-line rule clears just as well. No amount of instrument calibration detects
this. It requires asking, before running anything, what the simplest thing that
could produce this result would score -- and \citet{makridakis2018statistical}
show the field routinely does not ask.

\subsection{Errors in our own first revision}
\label{ssec:ourerrors}

Consistent with the argument above, we record the errors in the previous version
of this manuscript. This is not ceremonial: the paper argues that measurement
must be checked, and the checks found faults in our own corrected system.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Missing baseline.} Inverse persistence was not reported, despite
persistence being in the table and its inverse being one subtraction away. This
inverted the headline conclusion.
\item \textbf{Holding-period fault (F3).} Our corrected engine held positions for
two bars against a one-bar label.
\item \textbf{Wrong causal story.} The frictionless-to-executed gap was
attributed to a forfeited overnight gap. Crypto has no overnight gap; the
measured close-to-open dispersion is $1.6\%$ of intraday dispersion. Costs, not
timing, consume the edge.
\item \textbf{Arithmetic error.} Daily cost was stated as $14$ bps at $0.774$
turnover and $9$ bps per side; the product is $7.0$ bps. The stated figure also
contradicted the paper's own cost table.
\item \textbf{False precision.} A breakeven of $10.74$ bps was quoted to four
significant figures with no interval. The interval spans $[0, 26.9]$.
\item \textbf{Truncated panel.} The cost analysis silently discarded about $690$
days of BTC and ETH history by truncating to the shortest series.
\item \textbf{Uncalibrated flagship proposal.} The single-path null test fails on
a correct engine $13.3\%$ of the time.
\item \textbf{Overstated founding argument.} Equation~\eqref{eq:contradiction}
does not bind in the presence of timeout exits.
\item \textbf{Malformed benchmark comparison.} Raw Sharpe against long-only
buy-and-hold, where the appraisal ratio is the correct statistic.
\item \textbf{Inconsistent samples.} Three different row counts were quoted
across tables without reconciliation.
\item \textbf{A non-formula.} An expression purporting to relate gross Sharpe to
accuracy contained an undefined ``IR-adjustment'' term and was not dimensionally
coherent. It is removed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical against economic significance}

The pooled hit rate differs from $0.5$ at $p < 10^{-4}$; the gross Sharpe has a
bootstrap interval containing zero. Both are correct. Accuracy enters the
economics only through $2a - 1 = 0.059$, while costs enter as a subtraction
proportional to turnover, and the sample is short enough that a Sharpe of $0.54$
carries a standard error above $0.35$. Detecting an effect and being paid for it
are different questions with different power requirements, and the second is far
more demanding.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Report the trivial baseline first.} For directional forecasting,
persistence and its inverse cost one line each and bound the contribution of
everything else.
\item \textbf{Report a mutation score, not a test count.}
\item \textbf{Report breakeven cost with an interval}, not a net Sharpe under one
undisclosed assumption.
\item \textbf{Decompose gaps rather than explaining them.} Every step between two
reported numbers should be isolated and measured.
\item \textbf{Report $\mu$ and $\sigma$} so that every ratio is auditable.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
%==============================================================================

\paragraph{One audited system.} Our fault taxonomy comes from a single artefact.
We make no prevalence claim about the literature. The natural extension is a
survey of public repositories and published replication packages scoring each
against the checks proposed here; this would convert a rhetorical claim into an
empirical one and is not attempted.

\paragraph{Cost model.} We charge a flat $4$ bps taker fee plus $5$ bps slippage.
For a daily-horizon signal in BTCUSDT and ETHUSDT this is likely conservative:
top-of-book spread is well under a basis point with substantial depth, and a
daily strategy can work orders passively at maker fees that are lower and
negative at high volume tiers. A defensible passive-execution assumption of
$2$--$3$ bps per side would place the strategy above breakeven in the point
estimate. It would not change the conclusion, because the gross Sharpe interval
already contains zero -- but readers should understand that the cost assumption,
not merely the signal, is doing work. DOGE is the only leg where $9$ bps is
comfortably defensible.

\paragraph{Instrument idealisation.} We model Binance spot but evaluate a
long/short book, which requires margin. Funding costs, borrow availability and
liquidation risk are not modelled. In crypto perpetuals funding is frequently
paid \emph{to} shorts, so omitting it is not conservative in a known direction.

\paragraph{Overlapping observations.} Pooling across assets treats them as
independent; BTC and ETH daily returns are highly correlated, so the effective
sample is smaller than nominal and the pooled $p$-value is optimistic. The
per-asset results and the paired tests, which do not pool, are the conservative
reading and are what the conclusion rests on.

\paragraph{Feature scope.} Thirty-four single-asset technical features. Crypto
microstructure signals -- funding, open interest, basis, liquidations, order-book
imbalance, exchange flows, cross-asset lead--lag -- are absent. A negative result
on this feature set is not a negative result on the asset class.

\paragraph{Modelling machinery not exercised.} We use purging and embargoing from
\citet{lopezdeprado2018advances} but not meta-labelling, sample-uniqueness
weighting, sequential bootstrap, fractional differentiation or combinatorial
purged cross-validation. Meta-labelling in particular targets exactly the failure
we document in Table~\ref{tab:weighted}, where direction is right more often than
magnitude warrants.

\paragraph{Objective mismatch.} Models are trained on log-loss over direction,
not on a cost- or turnover-aware objective. If costs bind, they belong in the
loss.

\paragraph{Robustness not established.} We do not test sensitivity to the
$00{:}00$ UTC bar boundary, which for a daily-bar study of a $24/7$ market is a
genuine concern; re-anchoring at other hours would indicate whether the effect is
a boundary artefact. No regime analysis or structural-break test is reported.

\paragraph{No live validation.} No paper trading, capacity analysis or
size-dependent slippage.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

We audited a cryptocurrency trading system reporting an annualised Sharpe of
$4.54$, isolated twelve code-level faults from five root causes, and verified the
corrections by reintroducing each fault and confirming detection, achieving a
mutation score of $8/8$. Among the faults was a feature named for sentiment that
was a scaled copy of the contemporaneous label, correlating $0.916$ with the same
day return and $-0.018$ with the next.

Correcting the instrument was necessary and not sufficient. The re-measured
result -- $52.95\%$ directional accuracy over $5{,}562$ out-of-sample
predictions, $p < 10^{-4}$ -- is matched by inverse persistence at $52.88\%$, a
rule with no parameters. Paired tests on identical days give differences between
$-0.29$ and $+0.53$ percentage points with $|t| \le 0.45$, and the model loses on
two of three assets. Thirty-four features and walk-forward machinery recover a
documented one-day reversal effect and add nothing measurable to it. The effect
is not tradable on this feature set at this frequency: gross Sharpe is $0.544$
with a bootstrap interval of $[-0.17, +1.28]$, the appraisal ratio against a
passive basket is $-0.076$, and confidence gating degrades performance
monotonically.

The general lesson is that two independent things must be checked before a
backtested claim means anything. The instrument must be calibrated, which is
mechanical. And the baseline must be non-trivial, which is not mechanical and
which no amount of engineering discipline supplies. Our own first revision passed
the first test and failed the second, and we have documented that failure here
rather than quietly correcting it.

\section{Feature definitions}
\label{app:features}

All features are functions of bars indexed $\le t$. Let $O_t, H_t, L_t, C_t, V_t$
denote open, high, low, close and base volume, $Q_t$ quote asset volume, $N_t$
trade count and $B_t$ taker buy base volume.

\paragraph{Returns.} $\log(C_t / C_{t-k})$ for $k \in \{1,2,3,5,10,20\}$.

\paragraph{Volatility and location.} For $k \in \{5,10,20,60\}$, the rolling
standard deviation of one-day log returns and
$(C_t - \mu_k(C))/\sigma_k(C)$.

\paragraph{Bar shape.}
$(H_t - L_t)/C_t$, $(C_t - O_t)/O_t$, and $(C_t - L_t)/(H_t - L_t)$.

\paragraph{Order flow.} $B_t / V_t$ and its five-day mean; $Q_t / N_t$ and its
$60$-day $z$-score; $z$-scored $N_t$ and $V_t$; and $V_t$ over its $20$-day mean.

\paragraph{Technical.} RSI(14); MACD histogram
$(\mathrm{EMA}_{12} - \mathrm{EMA}_{26})$ minus its $9$-period EMA; Bollinger
position $(C_t - \mathrm{MA}_{20})/(2\sigma_{20})$; ATR(14) over $C_t$; and
$\sin(2\pi d/7)$, $\cos(2\pi d/7)$ for day of week $d$.

\section{Corrected execution model}
\label{app:execution}

The engine iterates over the union of bar timestamps. At each timestamp, for each
asset with a bar present, it resolves exits for any open position in \emph{that}
asset using \emph{that} bar, then opens positions scheduled for that bar, then
closes positions whose bar-counted holding period has expired at that bar close.

A signal timestamped within bar $i$ executes at bar $i+1$, filled at its open
adjusted by slippage $\varsigma$:
$p_{\text{entry}} = O_{i+1}(1 \pm \varsigma)$.

Cash and equity follow \eqref{eq:equity}. A long entry debits
$q\,p_{\text{entry}} + \varphi$; a short entry credits $q\,p_{\text{entry}} - \varphi$,
with $\varphi$ the fee on that leg's own notional. Exit resolution against a bar
uses $L_t$ and $H_t$, resolving pessimistically to the stop when a single bar
touches both levels, and otherwise closes at $C_t$ on holding expiry.

\section{Null-test procedure}
\label{app:null}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Randomised null test for a backtest engine}
\label{alg:null}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} engine $E$, seeds $M$, bars $n$, assets $J$, volatility $\sigma$, cost $c > 0$
\For{$m = 1$ to $M$}
  \For{each asset $j \in J$}
    \For{$t = 1$ to $n$}
      \State $P_t \gets P_{t-1}\exp(\sigma\varepsilon_t)$, \quad $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$
      \State emit bar consistent with $P_{t-1}, P_t$; with prob.\ $0.02$ emit a random-side signal
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State $(\mathcal{T}_m, V_m) \gets E(\text{signals}, \text{bars}, c)$; \quad $S_m \gets \mathrm{Sharpe}(V_m)$
  \State \textbf{assert} $V_{m,T} - V_{m,0} = \sum_i \mathrm{PnL}_{m,i}$ \Comment{cash conservation}
\EndFor
\State \textbf{assert} $\bar{S} / \mathrm{se}(S) < -3$ \Comment{distributional, not single-path}
\State \textbf{assert} realised fee rate $\approx$ modelled two-sided fee
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
